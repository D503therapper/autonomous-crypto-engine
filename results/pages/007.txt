The Lobos appeared to be on their chosen path with quarterback Jack Layne, who followed head coach Jason Eck and offensive coordinator Luke Schleusner from Idaho to UNM two years ago. Layne, who understood the language of the Lobos’ pro-style offense, completed a school-record 65.1% of his passes in 13 starts in 2024. Layne represented the Lobos at the league’s Media Days in July despite not throwing during spring workouts because of an elbow issue. He underwent a second surgery and was declared out for this season. The next-man-up competition began with Oregon transfer Luke Moga, second-year freshman Toa Fa‘avae and sixth-year James Laubstein. Fa‘avae has started all five games, with Moga getting reps in the first three. The left-handed Fa‘avae is a dual threat with speed (10.58 seconds over 100 meters), elusiveness (6.5 yards per non-sack scramble) and arm versatility, particularly off run-pass options and play-action throws. His snap-to-release average is 2.07 seconds on screens and hitches. He also has connected on 50% of his deep throws (20-plus yards airborne from line of scrimmage). The Lobos usually are in a one-back formation. When they go with double tight ends, Cade Keith (73.7% completions when targeted) usually sets up wide or part of a triangle formation. The Lobos play powerball. They have scored TDs on 45.6% of their full drives. They have cobbled eight drives of 10-plus plays. The weekly goal is 200-plus rushing yards, which they accomplished against every opponent except Oklahoma. The Lobos run 68.6% on first down, 70.2% on second, 65.6% on third down. On third-and-short, the call to rush is 90.1%. Two years ago, Montana State’s Scottre Humphrey ran for 140 yards in an upset of New Mexico. After being named an FCS All-American, Humphrey entered the portal. He joined the team he tormented. After an uneven 2025 season, Humphrey is the featured back. His long strides enhance his 4.53 speed. He also has the ability to get skinny in inside lanes; 67.3% of his rushes are between the guards. He averages 4.9 yards per first-down rush.

Defensive coordinator Spence Nowinsky is noted for cherry-red reading glasses and an aggressive 4-2-5 defense. The Lobos are the nation’s best with a plus-18 turnover margin (12 interceptions, seven fumbles recovered against one giveaway). Of opponents’ pass plays, 22.4% resulted in picks or sacks. The Lobos have 43 backfield tackles. The most disruptive is Darren Agu, who was born in Ireland but grew up in London. Agu, who transferred from Vanderbilt in 2025, plays the hybrid end/linebacker spot known as the “joker.” He made The Athletics’ Freak List because of these numbers: benched 385 pounds, soared 10-8 in the broad jump, touched 11-3 in the vertical jump, and raced 1.61 seconds in the 10-yard split. Agu has five TFLs, including three sacks. Brett Karhu, who plays the power end, has a team-high four sacks. Drew Speech and Jayden Flaker split reps at the “star” position, usually aligned in the middle of the three-across coverage. After his father was hired as UNM head coach, there was little doubt linebacker Jaxton Eck would join in the move from Idaho. Eck averaged 9.99 tackles per game last year and was named the Mountain West’s Co-Defensive Player of the Year. He is tied for the team lead with 25 tackles this season. But Eck’s streak of 32 consecutive games ends Saturday because of a shoulder injury. Mercury Swaim, who has made 17 in-the-box tackles, and Clay Martineau will take turns in the middle. Swaim gets the green dot on his helmet as the on-field communicator with the sideline.

Despite Kona Lows and tropical storms drenching the islands this year, the Warriors have experienced a scoring drought. They are averaging 6.3 first-half points. They have produced three TDs — two against New Mexico State — in 35 first-half drives. They are last among 138 FBS teams with 82.3 rushing yards per game. They are 1-for-7 on passes from the 10-yard line and closer, with wildcat Tama Uiliata throwing the lone TD to tight end Frank Abreu. The Warriors remain hopeful Cam Barfield, who recovered from an injury two weeks ago; DeVon Rice; and Sitani Mikaele, who scored 18 TDs as a Farrington senior, can resurrect the running game. Wideouts Audric Harris and Devin Alves have stretched defenses with go routes and posts. Slotback Titan Lacaden has emerged as a reliable receiver. He has caught 88.9% of targeted passes. After committing eight penalties (one was declined) against San Jose State, the O-line was not assessed any last week, but it still needs to provide more time for QB Micah Alejado, who has been sacked 17 times in 282 pass plays. The line has undergone some tinkering. Judah Kaio, who can play all three interior positions, is sharing play-calling with center Ethan Spencer. The tactic helps the line diagnose the defensive structure quicker. “It makes the calls and the IDs that much smoother,” Kaio said. With the keys and alerts delivered at a faster rater, the blockers can focus on their assignments and, perhaps, cut down on pre-snap penalties. In recent weeks, right guard Denaris DeRosa Jr. and right tackle Uluaki Taukiuvea worked their way into the lineup. Now Boogie Henderson, who opened as the left tackle, has worked his way back into the rotation. He said he regained a better perspective after taking mental reps. He also has found renewed motivation. Henderson and his girlfriend are expecting their first child in May. “It’s another thing that’s driving me,” Henderson said. “I have a family to provide for.”

As part of the wrinkles to the 4-2-5 defense last year, safety Peter Manuma moonlighted as the hawk, a linebacker-safety hybrid. Manuma completed his eligibility, and Julian “JuJu” Smith and Aisiah “Scoob” Paogofie have been trained as hawks. When the Warriors want to employ Smith or Paogofie as a third second-level defender, a D-tackle or defensive back is pulled. It was during the offseason that Elijah Palmer, who excels at nickel, wanted to find ways to remain as a three-down defender. “In previous years, he had been taken out on some of those packages,” safeties coach Nick Locher said of Palmer. “He didn’t want that for his senior year. We had some good conversations in the offseason about giving him those opportunities.” Palmer cross-trained as a safety, particularly in the deep middle. He also met with his mentor, associate head coach Chris Brown, on how to have hawk-like duties when the Warriors wanted to employ more run-stoppers. As co-captain, Palmer delivers the on-field pep talk ahead of practices and games. That vocal leadership has transcended to games. “He’s a great communicator,” Locher said. “That’s such a vital part of the position. When we do go to some of those other packages, he brings so much value in that role. It’s like, ‘Hey, we can’t take him off the field because he gets other people lined up and gets the defense in the right spots.’” Another vocal leader is D-end Josh Sagapolutele, who has made noise since moving from D-tackle. At 6 feet and 255 pounds, Sagapolutele uses his twitch — first-step quickness — and leverage to give fits to taller blockers. The coaches have noticed. After a practice three weeks ago, Sagapolutele was summoned to head coach Timmy Chang’s office. He was awarded a scholarship. “It’s such a blessing,” Sagapolutele said.
